5.38 million: the most people to live in Ireland since the Famine

The Republic's population is now estimated at its highest level since 1851. It has taken more than 170 years to undo the demographic collapse that began with the Great Famine — and the force now driving growth is migration, not birth.

Analysis · Data: CSO Census & Population Estimates · Updated 2024

No European country wears its demographic history as visibly as Ireland. In 1841, on the eve of the Great Famine, some 6.5 million people lived in what is now the Republic. Famine and a century of relentless emigration then halved it: by the early 1960s the population had fallen below 2.9 million, still draining away.

A population still below its pre-Famine peak
Total population by census year, millions.

It has taken 170 years to climb back — and Ireland is still short of its 1841 total.

The turn came in the 1960s, and growth has accelerated since. The 2022 census recorded 5.15 million; the CSO's 2024 estimate puts it at roughly 5.38 million — the fastest growth rate in the European Union.

Migration is now the engine

For most of the post-1960s recovery, growth came from natural increase — more births than deaths, a legacy of Ireland's historically young population. That has changed. In the year to April 2024, net inward migration reached an estimated 79,300, its highest in over a decade, and now accounts for the majority of annual population growth. Behind that net figure sits the highest gross churn on record: roughly 149,000 people arrived and 70,000 left.

That shift reshapes everything downstream: demand for housing, pressure on public services, the size of the workforce, and the country's cultural make-up. A nation defined for a century by the people it lost is now defined by the people it attracts.
